\subsection{Monotone generation and adaptive calibration}
\label{sec:monotone-adaptive}

A second paired pilot asks whether a smaller structural generator and
training-only weight selection can improve on fixed local calibration.
The monotone generator represents the log-valuation quantile as a linear
spline in $\Phi^{-1}(u)$, with eleven fixed knots, ten positive slopes, and
one intercept. Linear tail extensions remove the finite support imposed by
the original generator's $\tanh$ output. This is an eleven-parameter
generator rather than a larger network. Monotonicity gives the exact
distributional identity
\begin{equation}
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}Q_\gamma(U_i)
\overset{d}{=}Q_\gamma(U^{1/N}),
\qquad U,U_1,\ldots,U_N\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1),
\end{equation}
where the bidder draws are independent. The right-hand side generates
maxima directly. Local kernel moments use 32,768 deterministic midpoint
quantiles of this distribution. These are numerical integration nodes,
not additional observed auctions.

Each monotone generator starts from a data-only linear fit of log-maximum quantiles
on $\Phi^{-1}(p^{1/N})$. This initialization favors lognormal structure;
it is an eligible checkpoint, including step zero, rather than evidence
of a benefit from adversarial training. The original critic, adversarial
loss, gradient penalty, and 1,200 training steps are retained. The monotone
generator learning rate is $5\times10^{-4}$; the original network keeps
$2\times10^{-5}$. Architecture, tail extension, initialization, and
deterministic integration change together, so this pilot does not identify
their separate contributions.
The fixed spline dimension and linear normal-quantile tails also remain
approximation restrictions; unbounded support alone does not ensure tail accuracy.

For adaptive calibration, each outer training fold is split into an inner
fitting sample (75 percent) and a validation sample (25 percent). Choose
one shared CDF/density weight from $\{0,0.25,0.5,1\}$ using standardized
squared prediction errors of the observable kernel signals. Validation
uses 21 prices between 0.6 and 1.4 times the inner fitting-sample
direct-inversion reserve. Signal variances are estimated on that fitting
sample and floored at 0.01. The generator is then refitted to the entire
outer training fold. Only the untouched outer holdout enters its score.
The same selected weight applies at all prices for a given bandwidth;
different bandwidths may select different weights.

The four ablations vary generator (original or monotone) and mixing rule
(fixed or adaptive), with identical observations and outer folds. The
empirical-local DML and smoothed plug-in remain benchmarks. A lognormal
maximum-order-statistic MLE supplies a correctly specified parametric
benchmark in the lognormal design and a misspecified benchmark in the
Weibull design. Its sandwich delta-method interval targets the fitted
parametric reserve; under misspecification it need not cover the actual
reserve. Adaptive mixing does not inherit the $O(n^{-1})$ fixed-weight
argument: the local nuisance-rate and root assumptions require separate
verification. All exact-reserve intervals below remain diagnostic.

The evaluation design fixes $N=5$, $n\in\{500,2000\}$, and ten new
repetitions per cell. Valuations are either $\operatorname{LN}(0,0.5^2)$
or Weibull with shape 1.5 and scale one. Development uses seed 20260929;
final evaluation uses base seed 90260930 for lognormal and 20260930 for
Weibull, which has a separate distribution offset. An initial lognormal
cohort overlapped the prior calibration data because of additive seed
indexing. It is archived separately and replaced in full, with no estimator
or tuning changes, by the fresh lognormal cohort. No repetitions are
removed based on their outcomes. The exact
target uses the existing covariance-aware Richardson correction; the
replication archive also reports the fixed-bandwidth target separately.

In the independent lognormal $n=500$ cell, combined monotone/adaptive
RMSE is 0.0525, compared with 0.0593 for original fixed calibration,
0.0591 for empirical DML, and 0.0530 for the smoothed plug-in.
The paired MSE difference against the plug-in is $-0.000052$ with Monte
Carlo standard error $0.000214$, so the small ordering should not be
interpreted as a stable advantage. The correctly specified lognormal MLE is considerably more
accurate, with RMSE 0.0151, illustrating the information supplied by its
parametric restriction.

At lognormal $n=2000$, combined monotone/adaptive RMSE is 0.0318,
against 0.0335 for original fixed calibration and empirical DML, and
0.0349 for the smoothed plug-in. The paired MSE difference against the
plug-in is $-0.000207$ with Monte Carlo standard error $0.000236$.
Thus this cell also shows a favorable point estimate without resolving
the ranking. Correctly specified MLE RMSE is 0.0073. Adaptive mixing with
the original generator is worse than fixed calibration in all four cells;
weight selection by itself is not the source of an established gain.

The Weibull cells caution against treating validation-selected mixing as
an automatic improvement. At $n=500$, the combined monotone/adaptive
version has RMSE 0.0512 versus 0.0552 for original fixed calibration and
0.0503 for the smoothed plug-in. At $n=2000$, its RMSE rises relative to
these comparators: 0.0393 versus 0.0327 and 0.0328, respectively. Merely
replacing the generator while retaining fixed calibration closely tracks
the empirical estimator. The misspecified lognormal MLE has RMSE near
0.097 and zero observed actual-target coverage in both Weibull cells.
This illustrates parametric misspecification, but cannot establish a
WGAN-specific advantage because the empirical benchmarks also avoid it.

The fixed-calibration estimator therefore remains the main safeguarded
procedure. The adaptive variants are reported as experiments, not as a
validated replacement. Validation-based mixing and cross-fitting do
not ensure dominance in reserve-price error, and ten repetitions leave
substantial Monte Carlo uncertainty. The archive reports paired MSE
differences with their Monte Carlo standard errors rather than selecting
a method solely from the ordering of point estimates.
